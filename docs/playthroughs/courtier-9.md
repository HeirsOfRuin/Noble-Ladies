# courtier, seed 9

## Lent, 1400: Reputation

> A lady's good name is the first thing she owns and the last thing she should spend. It is built slowly, by many small proofs, and lost in an afternoon.

Two offers for your hand have come to your father, Messire Enguerrand de Brécourt. One is from Messire Jean de Rouvroy, lord of Fresnoy; the other from Messire Hue de Cramailles. Both houses will send men to the Easter feast in your father's hall, and those men will report on you. You are seventeen. Officially, none of this is your affair.

**Chose:** Talk well at table and let them see your wit

## May, 1400: Favour

> At court every kindness is a debt and every debt is remembered. Choose whose company you keep, because others will choose for you by watching it.

Your mother takes you to Paris for a month, to the Hôtel Saint-Pol and the great houses around it. The king is well this spring, and the court is full. Three circles of ladies will receive a girl of good family. You can only be seen to belong to one.

**Chose:** The queen's ladies

## June, 1400: Information

> Know a thing before you act on it, and know who told you. A report is worth what its bearer is worth.

Back home, you want to know what the two men are like, and nobody is going to tell you. Someone could find out.

**Chose:** Write to Ysabel de Fontenoy at court

## August, 1400: Persuasion through others

> Your father decides. That is the law and the custom. But a wise woman knows that decisions are made by people, and people can be moved.

Your father leans toward Messire Jean de Rouvroy, lord of Fresnoy: Fresnoy holds 13 holdings to Cramailles's 11, and asks no less in dowry. Your mother asks you, privately, which match you would choose if you could.

**Chose:** Tell her you will be content with your father's choice

## September, 1400: Persuasion through others

> Never ask for a thing yourself when someone he trusts can ask for it better. And never send someone who does not want it as much as you.

You have left the choice to your father, and he will take Fresnoy. But the terms are still open: how much he pays, and what he asks for you in return. Someone can speak to him about that.

**Chose:** Let your mother speak to him

## October, 1400: Consequences arrive late

> What you do today, you will meet again in five years, wearing different clothes. Choose as if you will have to live beside it.

At the queen's court for the feast of Saint Denis, Ysabel de Fontenoy and Jacqueline d'Aubercourt quarrel over who should walk first behind the queen's sister-in-law. Words are said. Both name you as a witness. You saw it all.

**Chose:** Speak for Ysabel

## December, 1400: The contract

> A marriage is a contract between two houses, and the contract is what protects you when the man does not. Read it. Have it read to you twice.

Your father has settled on Messire Jean de Rouvroy, lord of Fresnoy. You left it to him. A widower with no son, holding lands in Artois from the Duke of Burgundy. Rich, careful with money, short with people. The dowry agreed is 2400 livres. The wedding is set for the spring, after your eighteenth birthday.

The lawyers have drawn the contract in the usual form. In return for the dowry you are to swear that you will claim nothing more of your father's lands, now or after his death. Daughters do; few think about it. There is also the matter of your dower, should you be widowed.

**Chose:** Sign the terms as they stand

### April 1401 · The wedding

In April 1401, at eighteen, you marry Messire Jean de Rouvroy, lord of Fresnoy in the chapel at Brécourt, where your father and his father were married. Your father pays 2400 livres. Your husband holds 13 holdings and is known as a friend of the Burgundians. You leave your mother's house with Perrette, two chests, and the book of hours your mother gave you, with wide margins, "for your own thoughts, which nobody else will want."

- Act Two. The young wife at court, 1401 to 1407. You have a husband, a household and a name to build. The princes are already quarrelling.

**Chose:** Begin

### April 1401 · What you want

On the road to your husband's house your governess rides beside the litter for the first league, as far as the parish boundary. "Nobody will ask you what you want," she says. "So decide it now, while you can still hear yourself think, and do not tell anyone."

### Autumn 1401 · The first winter (Your husband)

Your first winter in his house. Fresnoy rises before light, hears mass, eats standing up and is gone until dark: horses, tenants, his lord's business.

He shouts at the grooms. Once, last week, he shouted at you, and then left the room as if he had embarrassed himself.

He checks the candle ends.

Tonight, by the fire, he asks what you did all day, in the voice of a man who expects nothing much.

**Chose:** Ask about his day, and listen

### Spring 1402 · The Romance of the Rose (Christine)

Christine de Pizan is at court with a bundle of letters tied in red. For a year she has been arguing in writing with Jean de Montreuil, the provost of Lille, and the Col brothers, one of them a secretary to the King, about the Romance of the Rose: Jean de Meun's great poem, which every gentleman at the Court of Love can quote, and which says that every woman is for sale and every wife a trap.

"They write ballades swearing to defend ladies' honour," she says, "and then they defend that book. I want the queen to read both sides. I cannot walk into her chamber. You can."

**Chose:** Carry the letters into the queen's chamber

### Spring 1402 · Seven against seven (Your husband's honour)

The Duke of Orléans is choosing seven knights to meet seven English knights in the lists at Montendre, in Saintonge, in May, for the honour of France and of the duke. Fresnoy wants to be one of them more than he has wanted anything since you married him. He needs new harness to be considered: Milanese, fitted, three hundred livres.

**Chose:** Tell him the harness is a year of your dowry

### Winter, 1407 · In the margins of her book of hours

I have said what I thought in great men's halls, and nobody has struck me dead yet. Fresnoy is what I was told he would be: no better, no worse, and away a good deal. Robert and Enguerrand are living, and 2 are in the churchyard. Master Lorfèvre says the Parlement is slow but not deaf. It has heard us three times, and Gérard still sleeps in my father's bed. In November they killed the Duke of Orléans in the street, and the whole city held its breath, and then let it out again. I lied for Ysabel before the queen's women. God forgive me, I would do it again. I carried Christine's letters against the Romance of the Rose into the queen's chamber. The King's secretary has not smiled at me since. Good. I know what the king said to the queen that night. I have never told anyone, and she knows it. I have a room in the Hôtel Barbette now, and the queen talks to me when she cannot sleep, which is most nights.

- What is said of her: bold, very warm.

**Chose:** Turn the page

## The end

Item, of Marguerite of the house of Brécourt, born in the year 1383, married at eighteen to Messire Jean de Rouvroy, lord of Fresnoy. She wanted a place among the queen's own ladies, and had it. Her lord came home from Agincourt, which few did. She bore 4 children, of whom 1 was living at the end. Robert was promised to the house of Ronquerolles. She made peace with her cousin over Brécourt by a marriage, so that her grandchildren would hold it. She brought her friend Ysabel de Fontenoy out of the Châtelet in the year of the butchers. She took in her enemy Jacqueline d'Aubercourt and her children when they had nowhere to go, and was repaid. She went with Queen Isabeau into exile at Tours, when few would. She kept her husband's oath to his brother-in-arms, Raoul de Fléchin, when it was dangerous to keep. Her lord trusted her judgment, and said so. Her house kept every holding it had, through all the troubles. She was a lady of middling report, and was called devout, very bold and very warm. She outlived the mad king, which was more than many managed.

"I sat with the queen through the worst nights of her life. Somebody had to."
